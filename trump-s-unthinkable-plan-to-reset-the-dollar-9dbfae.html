Trump's Unthinkable Plan to Reset the Dollar

Why this matters this week: mortgage rates just jumped to 7.45% even though most people think the Fed controls that number. It doesn't, and the real story is about who sets your borrowing costs and who benefits when inflation runs hot.

The situation in 60 seconds

The 30-year fixed mortgage rate jumped to 7.45% because the bond market, not the Fed, sets long-term borrowing costs, and the 10-year Treasury yield just hit 5.23%, its highest since 2007. The Fed raised its own short-term rate anyway, by a quarter point to 3.75-4%, but the argument here is that this won't cool inflation because today's price pressure comes from shortages (homes, chips, power) and from AI capital spending that doesn't slow down just because rates rise. Separately, the Treasury's own cash account at the Fed and a possible recalculation of a key inflation gauge are framed as a quiet way to make inflation look lower without prices actually falling.

Who wants what

Federal Reserve

Wants: Bring inflation down toward its 2% target without crashing the economy.

Leverage: Sets the short-term federal funds rate, but cannot directly control long-term yields or mortgage rates.

US Treasury

Wants: Manage the government's own borrowing costs and keep the financial system liquid.

Leverage: Controls the TGA, the Treasury's cash account at the Fed, which can inject or drain cash from bank reserves.

Bond market investors

Wants: Adequate compensation for the risk of lending to a heavily indebted US government.

Leverage: Set the 10-year Treasury yield through buying and selling, which banks use as the reference for 30-year mortgage pricing.

AI and tech companies

Wants: Cheap, abundant financing to keep building AI infrastructure.

Leverage: Enormous capital need, cited at $300-570B in new debt this year, that doesn't shrink when rates rise, according to Bill Ackman as quoted in the video.

Savers and pension holders

Wants: Purchasing power and promised benefits preserved over time.

Leverage: Little. Their returns are tied to official rates and inflation figures set by others.

The mechanism, visualized

Explain it to a friend in 30 seconds

"Everyone assumes the Fed sets mortgage rates. It doesn't. The Fed sets a short-term rate, but your 30-year mortgage actually tracks the 10-year Treasury yield, which investors set based on how much risk they think is in lending to the US government. That yield just hit 5.23%, the highest since 2007, and mortgages followed to 7.45%, with zero announcement."

"There's also a real debate about whether rate hikes even work right now, since a lot of today's inflation pressure is shortages and AI spending that barely reacts to rates, not simple excess demand."

"And it's worth naming, without assuming any secret plan, that a government carrying heavy debt is structurally better off if inflation runs a bit hot than if it has to pay high rates for a long time."

Check yourself

Who actually sets the 30-year mortgage rate, and why doesn't the Fed control it directly?

Bond market investors set the 10-year Treasury yield based on demand for US debt. Banks price mortgages off that yield. The Fed only sets short-term rates, which influence but don't dictate long-term borrowing costs.

Why might raising interest rates fail to cool inflation if the real problem is a shortage instead of too much demand?

Rate hikes work by cutting demand when "too much money chases too few goods." If the actual problem is too few homes, chips, or power, making borrowing pricier discourages building more of the scarce thing, which can keep prices up instead of bringing them down.

Why are some people skeptical that inflation gauges like Core PCE are being "recalculated"?

Statistics agencies do periodically revise how inflation is calculated, for stated methodological reasons, published openly. Skeptics point to the convenient timing for a heavily indebted government that wants to cut rates without admitting inflation is still high. But calling it a secret plan is the video's interpretation of motive, not a confirmed fact.
