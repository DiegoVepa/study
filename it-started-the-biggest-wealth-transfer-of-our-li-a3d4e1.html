The Biggest Wealth Transfer: Five Warning Signs vs Staying Invested

Oil, inflation, debt, AI spending and record valuations are colliding this week, and the historical record says panic usually costs more than it saves.

The situation in 60 seconds

Markets are sitting between two stories right now. On one side: oil risk from Iran tensions, inflation running ahead of wage growth, a $2 trillion federal deficit, hundreds of billions in AI infrastructure spending by Amazon, Alphabet and others, and valuation metrics like the Shiller CAPE near historic highs. On the other side: strong Q3 corporate earnings growth, a resilient jobs market, and a long historical pattern of stocks rising in the year after midterm elections. The debate isn't about whether the risks exist, it's about whether current earnings and growth justify the price being paid for future growth, especially from AI.

Who wants what

AI hyperscalers (Amazon, Alphabet, others)

Wants: Build out AI infrastructure fast enough to capture future demand.

Leverage: They control the capital and compute that set the pace of the entire AI buildout.

US Federal Reserve / Treasury

Wants: Manage inflation and the growing deficit without triggering a recession.

Leverage: Interest rate policy and bond issuance decisions.

Retail and institutional investors

Wants: Decide whether to stay invested through volatility or move to safer assets like treasury bonds.

Leverage: Collective buying and selling moves prices; herd behavior can amplify swings.

Oil markets / Iran

Wants: Geopolitical tension around Iran shapes global oil supply expectations.

Leverage: Oil price swings ripple directly into inflation and consumer spending.

US labor market / consumers

Wants: Keep wages rising and jobs available despite inflation pressure.

Leverage: Consumer spending drives roughly two-thirds of US economic activity.

Explain it to a friend in 30 seconds

"There's a bunch of scary stuff lined up right now: oil tensions with Iran, inflation beating wage growth, a two trillion dollar deficit, massive AI spending, and valuations near record highs by one measure. But earnings grew over 31% last quarter, which is actually why that same valuation measure, forward P/E, looks cheaper than its own 10-year average. And historically, stocks have gone up the year after every midterm election since 1946. The real danger isn't riding out a scary headline, it's panic-selling and missing the days the market snaps back, since missing just the 10 best days over 20 years can roughly cut your returns in half."
