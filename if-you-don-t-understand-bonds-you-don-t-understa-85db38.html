If You Don't Understand Bonds, You Don't Understand Money

Why this matters this week: the 10-year Treasury yield was around 5.08% as of October 20, 2026, and the yield curve just un-inverted after being flipped for two straight years. That single number is quietly setting your mortgage rate, your savings rate, and how much room the government has to spend.

The situation in 60 seconds

A bond is just a loan. You hand money to a government or a company, and they promise a fixed interest payment (the coupon) plus your principal back on a set date. Once that bond is issued, its payments never change, but its market price does. If new bonds start paying more, your old bond's fixed payment looks worse by comparison, so its price has to drop so a new buyer still gets a competitive return. That is the whole trick: bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. This mechanic sets mortgage pricing, savings account rates, pension fund health, and how cheaply governments can borrow, because it is the base price of money across the entire economy.

Who wants what

US Treasury / federal government

Wants to borrow cheaply to cover the gap between spending and tax revenue.

Leverage: can issue as much debt as investors will buy, backed by taxing power and reserve-currency status.

Bond investors (pensions, insurers, individuals)

Want predictable income and a return that compensates for risk and time.

Leverage: collectively set prices and yields through what they choose to buy, acting like a real-money opinion poll.

Federal Reserve

Wants to manage inflation and growth through short-term interest rates.

Leverage: sets the fed funds rate, which anchors short-term Treasury yields and shapes the whole yield curve.

Corporations issuing bonds

Want to fund expansion or refinance debt without diluting shareholders.

Leverage: credit rating determines how cheaply they can borrow relative to Treasuries.

Mortgage borrowers and savers

Want low borrowing costs and decent returns on cash.

Leverage: very little individually; they are price-takers reacting to Treasury yield moves.

How we got here

  • Late 2017Fed officials publicly debated whether a narrowing spread between long- and short-term yields signaled a coming slowdown.
  • Late 2021 to early 2022The Fed signaled it would start raising rates, pushing bond yields up across the curve.
  • March 2022The Fed raised its target rate and pointed to further hikes, directly repricing short-term Treasury yields.
  • Mid-2022 to mid-2024The yield curve stayed inverted for an extended stretch, the classic recession-watch signal.
  • August 2026The 10-year minus 2-year spread normalized to an upward slope of about +0.51%, ending the inversion without an official recession having occurred.

Claims, sorted by how solid they are

Established

When interest rates rise, existing bond prices generally fall, and vice versa.

This is basic bond-pricing math: a fixed coupon competing against newer, better-paying bonds. Not a disputed theory.

Established

An inverted yield curve is a widely cited near-term predictor of recessions.

Sourced to Federal Reserve Board research. Historically, inversions preceded most past US recessions, though timing and certainty are never guaranteed.

Established

Prepayment risk explains roughly 80% of the gap between mortgage rates and the 10-year Treasury yield.

Attributed to a specific Boston Fed research paper. A researched estimate, not a universal consensus figure.

Contested

The flattening or inverted curve of 2017 to 2018 meant a downturn was coming soon.

Even the Fed's own officials disagreed at the time: some read it as a warning, others as a normal side effect of rate hikes.

Contested

The 2022 to 2024 inversion's predictive power for recessions.

That inversion lasted two years and never produced an official US recession, which has fed an active debate about whether the old signal still works the same way.

Speaker's view

Bond yields work like a constantly updating economic opinion poll backed by real money.

A framing device from the video's narrator to make yields feel intuitive. Not a claim sourced to any institution.

The mechanism that makes it click

Explain it to a friend in 30 seconds

"A bond's payment never changes, but its price does. Say you hold a $1,000 bond paying 3%, that's $30 a year. If new bonds suddenly pay 5%, nobody wants your old one at full price, so its price has to drop to around $600, because $30 divided by $600 is 5%. The promise wasn't broken, the market just repriced it. Now flip it: when short-term bonds pay more than long-term ones, that's called an inverted yield curve, and it's historically preceded most US recessions, because investors are betting the Fed will have to cut rates later as growth slows."

Worth sitting with: if short-term bonds are now paying more than long-term ones, what does that tell you investors expect the Fed to do next?

Check yourself

Why does an old bond's price fall when rates rise, even though its payments never change?

The bond's fixed coupon stays the same, but new bonds now pay more for the same money, so the old bond must get cheaper to offer a competitive return. The promise wasn't broken, the market just repriced it.

What does each side actually want: the government, the investor, and the Fed?

The government wants cheap borrowing to cover spending without raising taxes. Investors want a return matched to the risk and predictable income. The Fed wants to influence short-term rates to manage inflation and growth, which feeds into the whole yield curve.

Why is an inverted yield curve treated as a recession warning?

Normally long-term bonds pay more because more can go wrong over more years. When short-term yields pay more than long-term, investors are betting the Fed will cut rates later because growth is weakening. This pattern has preceded most past recessions, but it's a warning sign, not a certain forecast.
