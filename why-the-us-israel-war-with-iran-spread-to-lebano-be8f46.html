Why the US-Israel war with Iran spread to Lebanon

A weakened Hezbollah fired anyway, Israel hit back hard, and Lebanese civilians are paying for a fight they didn't start again.

The situation in 60 seconds

A ceasefire that followed the brutal 2024 Israel-Hezbollah war had held, barely, until Hezbollah fired rockets into Israel. Israel answered with strikes on Beirut's southern suburbs, south Lebanon, and the Beqaa Valley. The 2024 war had already wiped out much of Hezbollah's top command and weapons stockpile, so this time it cannot match Israel's firepower or technology. Hundreds of thousands of Lebanese civilians were ordered to evacuate again, even though much of the country still hasn't rebuilt from the last war. Inside Lebanon, more people are now saying it should be the national army, not Hezbollah, that controls the south.

Who wants what

Israel

Wants: End rocket attacks from Lebanon and degrade Hezbollah's remaining military capacity.

Leverage: Overwhelming military and technological superiority, already proven by crippling Hezbollah's command in 2024.

Hezbollah

Wants: Keep its role as an armed resistance force and retaliate against what it calls a ceasefire violation.

Leverage: Remaining rocket stockpile and political influence inside Lebanon, backed by Iran.

Iran

Wants: Keep a proxy foothold on Israel's northern border as leverage in its wider regional conflict.

Leverage: Funding and training of Hezbollah, plus similar proxy networks in Syria, Yemen, and Iraq.

Lebanese government

Wants: Avoid further destruction and regain exclusive state control over military force.

Leverage: Limited; can offer aid and diplomatic condemnation but lacks the power to disarm Hezbollah.

Lebanese civilians

Wants: Safety, rebuilt homes, and an end to repeated displacement.

Leverage: Growing public pressure and calls for Hezbollah's disarmament, which shapes domestic political legitimacy.

How we got here

1980s

Hezbollah is established during Israel's invasion of Lebanon, backed by Iran.

2024

A major Israel-Hezbollah war badly damages Hezbollah's command structure and weapons stockpile; much of Lebanon's infrastructure is destroyed.

After 2024

A ceasefire holds, but rebuilding stalls as Lebanon faces a financial and banking crisis.

Early March 2026

Hezbollah launches rockets into Israel, breaking the ceasefire.

Following days

Israel strikes Dahiyeh, south Lebanon, and the Beqaa Valley, some without warning; mass evacuation orders hit roughly 300,000 people in Dahiyeh and 250,000 near Tyre.

March 4, 2026

Hezbollah leader Naim Qassem publicly defends the fight as legitimate and accuses Israel of violating the ceasefire.

The core mechanism

Explain it to a friend in 30 seconds

"So back in 2024 Israel and Hezbollah fought a war that basically wrecked Hezbollah's leadership and weapons. They agreed a ceasefire, but Lebanon never fully rebuilt. Then Hezbollah fired rockets into Israel anyway, and Israel hit back hard, bombing Beirut's suburbs, the south, and the Beqaa Valley. Because Hezbollah's already so weakened, it can't really fight back on equal terms. Hundreds of thousands of Lebanese civilians had to evacuate again, and a lot of Lebanese are now fed up, saying the army should control the south, not Hezbollah. It's really an Iran problem playing out on Lebanese soil."

Check yourself

Why couldn't Hezbollah mount a serious military response this time?

The 2024 war eliminated top command and destroyed much of its weaponry, leaving it unable to match an established army's technology.

Why is Hezbollah described as a proxy rather than an independent militia?

It was founded in the 1980s during Israel's invasion and has been trained and funded by Iran ever since, acting on Iran's behalf in Syria, Yemen, and Iraq too.

Explain in 30 seconds why fighting in Lebanon flared up again in 2026.

A strong answer covers: a 2024 ceasefire that left Hezbollah weakened but intact, Hezbollah firing rockets anyway, Israel's heavy retaliation on Beirut's suburbs and the south, mass evacuations, and growing Lebanese frustration that one armed group can drag the whole country into war.

Common mix-up: this is not a simple Israel-versus-Lebanon conflict. Lebanon's own government condemned Hezbollah's rocket attacks, meaning the state and the armed group inside it are not aligned.
