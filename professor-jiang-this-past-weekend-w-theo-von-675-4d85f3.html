The Iran Escalation Ladder

Why a high school teacher's war-game prediction matters this week

Professor Jiang, a geopolitics teacher who says his game theory called Trump's 2024 win, laid out a step by step US-Iran war scenario on Theo Von's podcast. Since late February 2026 a real US-Israel conflict with Iran has followed a similar pattern, and this week Iran again disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.

The situation in 60 seconds

A high school teacher who runs a geopolitics YouTube channel, known as Professor Jiang, uses game theory to predict how conflicts unfold: identify the rules and incentives each side faces, then work out their best strategy. He says this method let him correctly call Trump's 2024 win and, two years ahead, a US war with Iran. On the podcast he walks through a four-step "escalation ladder": a decapitation strike on Iran's leadership, a naval blockade of its oil exports, threatened strikes on power infrastructure, and Iran activating proxy groups like Hezbollah and the Houthis. Each harsher step only gets used once the one below it fails to force surrender. He also explains why two narrow shipping lanes, the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el Mandeb Strait, put global oil, food, and fertilizer trade at risk from the same conflict, and traces today's US-Israel-Gulf alignment back to a 1996 Israeli strategy memo called Clean Break.

Who wants what

United States

Wants: Force Iran's capitulation and protect oil flow and allies without absorbing full-scale war costs.

Leverage: Military strikes, naval blockade capability, alliance network with Gulf states.

Iran

Wants: Preserve the regime, resist decapitation, make the war too costly for the US and its allies to continue.

Leverage: Proxies (Hezbollah, Houthis, Iraqi militias), ability to disrupt Hormuz shipping, a higher tolerance for economic pain.

Israel

Wants: Eliminate Iran-aligned threats like Hezbollah and secure the region through strength rather than negotiation.

Leverage: Military strikes, intelligence influence over regional leaders.

Saudi Arabia and Gulf states

Wants: Keep oil exports flowing and avoid direct war while protecting energy infrastructure.

Leverage: Economic ties to the US economy, potential ground action against the Houthis.

Proxy groups (Hezbollah, Houthis, Shia militias)

Wants: Protect Iran's interests and keep local legitimacy through services.

Leverage: Control over secondary chokepoints like Bab el Mandeb, ability to raise the war's global cost.

Check yourself

Why does the speaker argue Iran can absorb economic pain better than the US can?

Because decades of sanctions already normalized scarcity for Iranians, while sudden cost spikes would trigger unrest among Americans unused to that kind of hardship.

Why would closing the Strait of Hormuz threaten Africa's food supply, not just Asia's oil supply?

Because fertilizer, a byproduct of oil production, also transits Hormuz and feeds African agriculture, so closing it threatens both energy and food chains at once.

Explain the escalation ladder idea in your own words. What should a good answer cover?

Conflicts escalate in a logical order rather than randomly; each side tries the cheapest coercive tactic first (decapitation strike, economic blockade, infrastructure strikes, proxy activation); and the next rung only gets used once the previous one fails to force surrender.

Common misconception: Russia and Ukraine do not supply a third of the world's carbohydrates. The accurate figures are about 12% of internationally traded food calories and 28 to 30% of globally traded wheat specifically.

Worth discussing:

If Iran can tolerate economic pain better than the US because of decades of sanctions, what actually forces either side to back down first?
